We can now build and record from biological neural networks at increasing resolution and scale: new technologies let us read from thousands of electrodes at once and write information back in through electrical stimulation. In collaboration with PhD candidate Ash Robbins, I helped build the first closed-loop system to show goal-directed learning in cortical organoids. We coupled an organoid to a simulated pole-balancing task in which one set of neurons received stimulation encoding the pole's angle while the firing of a separate set was decoded into actions to balance the cart. The network performed well above a random-action control. What it does not reveal is what changed inside the network as it learned, because a system that tracks only performance leaves the network's internal activity a black box. Reading that activity requires a description of a network's dynamics that holds across independent networks rather than fitting any single recording; no such description currently exists for these systems due to the different idiosyncrasies of organoids such as dimensionality of organoid recorded units.

Two long-standing theories of learning predict different answers: instruction holds that the training signal writes new structure into the network, while selection holds that the patterns a task needs are already present in its spontaneous activity, recruited and amplified rather than created. These are ends of a continuum, and where biological learning falls along it is a question no one has read directly. To build a unified ruler that can extend to unseen organoids, I will build a foundation model of neural dynamics, trained to predict activity across large volumes of electrophysiological recording so that its internal representation becomes a compact, transferable description of how these networks evolve over time. Pretraining a single model across many recordings to yield representations that transfer to unseen networks is established for neural population data (Azabou et al., 2025), though it has only been applied to mature brain recordings, not in vitro networks. I will validate the model by predicting activity in held-out organoids it never saw in training so that any transfer is attributable to learned dynamics rather than the recording setup.

The closed-loop system drives an organoid toward a task and records the whole process at high resolution. I can then project that activity into the model's high-dimensional space, where learning becomes a measurable trajectory. I first map the baseline geometry of spontaneous activity: the region the network occupies on its own and how it moves through it. I then map successful performance and project the learned state against that baseline. To the degree the task-performing state lies inside the spontaneous repertoire, and the network visits it unprompted, learning is selecting from structure that was already there. To the degree the network reaches it only once driven, and the learned state departs from that spontaneous structure, the training signal is writing in something new. This turns a long-running debate about how learning works into a quantity I can read off as geometry.

Composition shapes what a network brings to a task before any learning begins. In our goal-directed work dorsalized forebrain organoids could learn to balance the pole, but performance was variable and often modest. This suggests the substrate, not just the training signal, limits what the network achieves. Separately, I have shown that ventralized forebrain organoids, enriched for interneurons, develop more modular activity and higher theoretical information transmission than their interneuron-poor dorsalized counterparts. These results motivate a direct test: train both compositions on the same task and ask how composition affects how well they learn and where their learned states sit relative to the spontaneous repertoire. A selection account makes a specific prediction. If learning recruits activity the network already generates, then a richer, more modular repertoire should make more of the task-relevant activity available to begin with, so ventralized networks should learn more consistently and to a higher ceiling. The same geometry tells me whether the repertoire, rather than the training signal, is what explains that difference: for each network, I can measure how far its task-performing state sits from the spontaneous repertoire, quantified as its probability under the network's own pre-task activity. Two signatures would point to selection. Better-performing networks should reach states their own spontaneous activity already places nearby, and this relationship should hold across networks rather than in a single case. The interneuron-rich ventralized networks should show it most strongly, because the feature that raises their information transmission is the same one that places task-relevant activity within easy reach.

Beyond its results, this work leaves reusable infrastructure. Other labs could fine-tune the model on their own recordings and probe learning in any engineered network, since the closed-loop software is open sourced and the model integrated within the standardized structure. By reading the geometry of a living network as it learns, the project brings one of neuroscience's most enduring questions within reach: whether the brain recruits the activity it already generates or has its solutions written in from outside. The same coordinate system would let us ask how shifts in cellular composition, including imbalances seen in disease, reshape what a network can learn.
